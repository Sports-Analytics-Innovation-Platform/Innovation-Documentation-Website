No code, just the numbers to reason about. A few real constraints pull in
different directions here, so it's a genuine tradeoff, not a single right
answer.

**What the models themselves need:**
- Elo works with any amount of history — starts noisier, sharpens as
  games accumulate. No hard minimum.
- Four Factors regression has a real cliff: MINIMUM_GAMES_FOR_REGRESSION =
  30 leaguewide games before it stops using the heuristic fallback. Already
  well past that (243 games).
- But 30 leaguewide isn't 30 per team — with ~15-22 games/team, each
  team's own running average is still a fairly small, noisy sample. Real
  Four Factors work assumes closer to a full season (82 games) per team.

**What "flooding the database" actually costs:**
- Storage-wise, basketball data is small — a full season for all 30 teams
  is maybe 30-35k rows total. Not a storage problem.
- The real cost is ingestion time/API load — each additional game per team
  is another BoxScoreTraditionalV3 call, rate-limited to ~1/second. Going
  from ~15 games/team to a full 82-game season is roughly a 5-6x increase
  in boxscore calls.

**Recommendation:** don't jump straight to a full season. A reasonable
middle step is 30-40 games per team — enough that each team's own running
average is meaningfully less noisy, keeps ingestion runtime manageable,
without fabricating precision you don't have data to support. Full-season
(82) is the "most accurate in principle" answer, but treat that as a later
step once you've confirmed the model's actually worth that investment —
which loops back to wanting a backtest, since right now you'd be tuning
"more data" against a number you haven't measured yet.

Good question to separate into two parts — data volume for the finished
product, and genuinely new modeling ideas beyond what's already built.

## Data volume, for the finished product (not the demo)

For something you'd actually stand behind as accurate, not just
methodologically sound: a full season per team (82 games) is the real
target, not a compromise. That's what Four Factors research and Elo both
assume as their baseline unit of "enough history." At that point:
- Ingestion becomes a genuinely long-running job (worth running once and
  re-running incrementally, not fetching everything from scratch every
  time — the ingestion script would benefit from only pulling new games
  since the last run rather than always taking "most recent N")
- You'd want this to run on a schedule (e.g. nightly) rather than
  manually, once it's not a one-off demo dataset

That's a real infra decision, not just a GAMES_PER_TEAM bump — worth
flagging now rather than discovering it later.

## Additional modeling ideas from the literature

Beyond what's already implemented (Elo, Four Factors), here's what real
sports-analytics work adds next, roughly in order of how well-established
and tractable each is:

**Rest and schedule effects** — the most robustly documented factor not
currently in your model. Back-to-back games, 3-games-in-4-nights
stretches, and long road trips have measurable, repeatedly-studied effects
on NBA performance. Computable purely from gameDate (already have it) — no
new data source needed.

**Home/away split in Four Factors, not just Elo's flat home-court bonus**
— right now home-court advantage only shows up in Elo's fixed
HOME_COURT_ADVANTAGE_ELO constant. Real models often let a team's own home
vs. away shooting/turnover splits vary, since some teams have a much
bigger home boost than others.

**Strength-of-schedule adjustment** — a team's Four Factors average right
now treats every opponent as equal. Real efficiency-based models (like
Basketball-Reference's SRS, or Ken Pomeroy's college-basketball approach)
adjust for opponent quality — beating a bad defense inflates your raw
eFG% without saying much about how you'd do against a good one.

**Possession-based pace adjustment** — Four Factors as implemented is
rate-based already, which avoids the most basic pace trap, but a fuller
model would separately estimate each team's pace (possessions per game)
and blend that with efficiency to predict actual scoring totals, not just
margin.

**Ensemble/blend of Elo and Four Factors** — right now they're two
independent models answering two different questions (win probability vs.
margin) that never talk to each other. A more sophisticated version would
blend Elo's rating gap into the margin prediction (or vice versa), closer
to what FiveThirtyEight's real model does (blending a rating-based model
with a box-score-based one).

**Player-level injury/availability** — still the biggest realistic gap,
still the hardest to source (no clean free injury-report API), so still
ranked last despite being high-impact.

I'd prioritize rest/schedule effects as the next concrete addition — it's
well-documented, doesn't need new data, and is a bounded, testable piece
of work, unlike strength-of-schedule or ensembling which are bigger
methodology shifts.
